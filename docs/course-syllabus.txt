Course Description

The first wave of data science focused on accuracy and efficiency: on what we can do with data. The second wave is about responsibility: what we should and should not do. This course aims to give future AI professionals the technical skills and the socially grounded understanding necessary to incorporate algorithmic fairness, transparency, and responsible data engineering objectives into the design of AI systems, alongside topics in technology ethics and legal compliance. 

The course is organized into five modules: an introduction to the hallmarks of responsible AI and to competing stakeholder values; algorithmic fairness, including fairness in classification and in risk assessment; the responsible use of data across the lifecycle, from profiling and cleaning to responsible model selection; transparency and interpretability, including black-box auditing, SHAP and LIME; and AI safety, covering generative AI, its risks and harms, and the emerging legal and regulatory landscape. Weekly labs pair each topic with hands-on work in Python using open-source tooling, and the course culminates in a hands-on project in which small teams of students conduct an audit of an AI system used in decision-making.

The course is structured into a sequence of lectures, hands-on labs, and accompanying assignments.  The assignments include labs, quizzes, homeworks, and a course project. This course uses the python programming language. All labs and homework assignments are expected to be formulated as notebooks in Google Colaboratory. 

While none of the homeworks are stand-alone writing assignments, writing is embedded in the homeworks. During the homeworks, students write computer programs and provide short essays on the interpretation of the data and the implications for the data around some decision or problem that the data informs. The writing length would typically be one to two pages for these essays.

Learning Outcomes

After successfully completing the course, students will develop a nuanced understanding of different responsible data science objectives, and will learn to communicate them to others. They will be able to use this understanding to develop technical interventions, and to critique and improve interventions developed by others. Specifically, students will be able to:

Explain that AI impacts different stakeholders differently, that stakeholders’ values and goals may be at odds with each other, and that technical choices are also normative choices: there is rarely one correct way to implement a technical method, because of the multitude of objectives, some of which may be incompatible.

Articulate the differences between various interpretations of algorithmic fairness, and relate these interpretations to the points of view of different stakeholders.

Reason about the lifecycle of data and models holistically when evaluating the impacts of technical design choices, recognizing that lifecycle stages impact each other in complex ways and usually cannot be cleanly decoupled.

Implement a computer program that assesses the impact of data preprocessing operations on different aspects of the performance of a model trained on the preprocessed data.

Implement a computer program that explains the decisions of a classifier, and a program that audits a black-box classifier.

Explain the role of ethical principles and legal frameworks, including data protection law and emerging AI-specific regulation, in the responsible design, development, and use of AI.

Communicate the results of an audit of an AI system used in decision-making to technical and non-technical audiences, articulating trade-offs in performance, fairness, and interpretability in view of different stakeholders and their values, and recognize that technologists play an important role in whether and how AI systems are designed, developed and used, and consequently carry substantial professional responsibility for the work they do.

Describe how generative AI systems are built, the risks and harms they introduce, and the safety and governance mechanisms that apply to them.

Course assessment
All individual assignments (labs, quizzes, homeworks, and the written reflection) must be the student's own work, completed in accordance with the course AI use policy adopted by the class in Lab 1. Any use of AI tools must be disclosed as required by that policy. Undisclosed AI use, and any sharing or copying of solutions between students, is considered cheating and will result in an F in the course. A second cheating incident will result in a one-semester suspension from the program. Project deliverables are the joint work of the project team and are subject to the same AI use policy.

Students accumulate up to 100 points during the course:

Labs: 10 points. Includes participation credit for the Lab 1 AI use policy activity.

Quizzes: 6 quizzes, best 5 count, 4 points each = 20 points.

Homeworks: 3 assignments × 10 points = 30 points.

Team project: 20 points (proposal: 2, part 1 presentation: 3, part 1 report: 5, final report: 10).

Final exam: 20 points.

Course Policies

Attendance and Tardiness: Attendance is not graded. However, quizzes are given in class, as announced in advance, and students who arrive after a quiz has ended cannot take it. The quiz policy (the best five of six scores count) is designed to absorb one missed quiz, for any reason, with no makeup needed. This course is strongly cumulative.  For example, the transparency and interpretability material of weeks 9–11 assumes fluency with the fairness and data lifecycle material that precedes it, and the course project draws on all of it. Students who fall behind rarely recover without help. Two absences may be taken without explanation. Students who miss a session should review the posted slides and lab notebooks, work through that week's materials, and bring what remains unclear to office hours. No medical documentation is requested for any absence. The one session where attendance is strictly required is the Lab during week 12 (Fri, 11/20), when  project presentations will take place. A team member who cannot present must arrange this with the instructor in advance.

Late Assignments: Each student has two late days for the semester, to be used on the homework assignments at the student's discretion, with no permission or explanation needed. The two late days form a single budget across all three homeworks: a student may use none at all, one day on each of two homeworks, or both days on a single homework. Once the two late days are exhausted, no late days remain for the rest of the semester. Late days are counted in whole days from the deadline: a homework submitted even one hour late has used one late day, and at the 24-hour mark it begins using the second. A homework submitted after a student's late days are exhausted, or more than two days after the deadline, receives no credit. Late days apply only to the three homework assignments; no other assignment may be submitted late. Quizzes cannot be made up; the drop policy (the best five of six count) absorbs a missed quiz, and the project deliverables are due as scheduled, with project deadlines shared by the whole team.

Artificial Intelligence Statement

This course treats the responsible use of AI as part of its subject matter and as a matter of classroom practice. During the first lab, held asynchronously on the course discussion board, the class deliberates on and adopts the course AI use policy. Course staff publish a starter policy in which some elements are fixed and explained (quizzes and the final exam are AI-free, and disclosure of AI use is always required), while several decisions are genuinely open: how much AI is permitted in homework code and in written work, what a disclosure must contain, and how students are asked to explain their work. These are discussed in each lab section and voted on by the class. Where sections disagree, the instructors decide and explain their reasoning. The adopted policy is presented in lecture, posted on Brightspace, and governs the rest of the semester. Participation in the deliberation counts toward the lab grade.

Every individual assignment, including all three homework assignments, requires an AI-use disclosure in the format the class adopts, stating which tools were used and how. Project deliverables are subject to the same policy. Undisclosed AI use is treated as cheating under the academic honesty policy above and will result in an F in the course.
